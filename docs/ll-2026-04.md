# LL-2026-04, and what Custody actually claims

Fannie Mae Lender Letter LL-2026-04, *Governance framework on use of artificial
intelligence and machine learning*, was issued 8 April 2026 and took effect
**6 August 2026** (120 days later). It applies to seller/servicers using AI/ML in
connection with originating loans sold to or guaranteed by Fannie Mae, or
servicing loans on Fannie Mae's behalf.

Primary source:
<https://singlefamily.fanniemae.com/news-events/lender-letter-ll-2026-04-governance-framework-use-artificial-intelligence-and-machine-learning>

Read the letter. Do not rely on this document, and in particular do not rely on
vendor summaries of it — including the ones that circulated a specific
eight-field audit-record schema the letter does not contain.

---

## What the letter requires

Quoted and paraphrased from the letter itself. To the extent a seller/servicer
uses AI/ML in connection with originating loans sold to or guaranteed by Fannie
Mae, or servicing loans on Fannie Mae's behalf, it must ensure compliance with
applicable laws and the Lender Contract, and in addition must:

1. **Have policies and procedures** regarding the development, implementation,
   use and maintenance of any AI/ML system it utilizes, and the measuring and
   managing of AI/ML risks. At a minimum those must:
   - be transparent and communicated to appropriate personnel who have job
     responsibilities in areas that use AI/ML;
   - incorporate the characteristics of trustworthy and ethical AI/ML;
   - reflect an understanding of legal and regulatory requirements regarding
     AI/ML;
   - reflect risk management activities **based on the lender's risk tolerance**;
     and
   - **have an owner or owners** who implement, maintain and review the policies
     and procedures **at least annually**, to ensure they comply with applicable
     law and consistently reflect industry best practices.
2. **Comply with the Fannie Mae Information Security and Business Resiliency
   Supplement.**
3. **Manage risks and appropriate governance of subcontractor and vendor use of
   AI/ML that is no less protective** of these requirements.

And, **upon request by Fannie Mae**, promptly disclose the types of AI/ML used,
the purpose and manner for such use, the safeguards implemented to mitigate
risks related to that use, and such other information as Fannie Mae may require.

Effective 120 days from publication: **6 August 2026**.

## What the letter does not require

It does **not** specify a per-decision record schema. It does not name the fields
`principal, model, endpoint, redacted prompt, response treatment, policy version,
decision outcome, timestamp`. It does not require append-only storage,
cryptographic signing, or a log queryable by loan, date, model and principal.

Those are one implementation of obligations 1 and 4 — a reasonable one, and the
one this library takes — but they are Custody's design choices, not Fannie Mae's
instructions. Any vendor telling you the letter mandates them is selling you
something.

## What Custody actually claims

The letter regulates seller/servicers, not tools. Custody cannot be compliant
with it; a lender can be. What follows is what Custody does and does not do
against each clause, so that a lender can judge the gap for themselves.

**The clause it was built for is the last paragraph.** A lender can be asked, at
any time, to promptly disclose what AI it runs, for what purpose and in what
manner, and what safeguards are in place. There are two ways to answer: a
document describing what is supposed to happen, or a record of what did happen
that the person asking can verify without taking your word for it.

| Clause | What Custody does | Honest limit |
|---|---|---|
| Applicable law and the Lender Contract | Nothing | Out of scope |
| Policies and procedures exist | Evaluates a versioned policy document and enforces it before the model is called; refusals are recorded | **You still write the policy.** Custody enforces what you approved and can say nothing about whether it is the right thing to have approved |
| Transparent, communicated to personnel | Nothing | Organisational |
| Characteristics of trustworthy and ethical AI/ML | Serves accountability, transparency, explainability (every verdict carries its reason) and privacy (redaction before persist) | Nothing for fairness or harmful-bias management |
| Understanding of legal/regulatory requirements | Nothing | Organisational |
| **Risk management based on the lender's risk tolerance** | The confidence floor and which checks are fatal are set per policy -- that is a risk-tolerance dial -- and the setting in force is recorded on every decision | Choosing the right tolerance is your judgement |
| **Owner reviews the policies at least annually** | `policy_version` on every record makes "which decisions ran under the old policy" a query rather than an excavation; the policy names an owner and a `last_reviewed` date, and an overdue one is reported in every packet and on every run | The owner and the review are organisational. Custody can only make the omission visible |
| Information Security and Business Resiliency Supplement | **Unassessed.** Custody is a data store holding redacted prompts and decision outcomes, so it is in scope of that Supplement and has not been evaluated against it | A real open item, flagged rather than glossed |
| **Vendor/subcontractor AI/ML governance no less protective** | **Custody uses no AI/ML.** The gate is deterministic and there is deliberately no model anywhere in the verification path, so adopting it adds no new AI/ML vendor surface to govern | It is still a vendor for every other purpose |
| **On request: types of AI/ML used** | Distinct models across the ledger, queryable | Only what was routed through Custody |
| **On request: purpose and manner of use** | The `purpose` field and redacted prompt on every decision, per loan and in order | As above |
| **On request: safeguards implemented** | The gate rules *and every verdict as evidence they ran* -- including the rejections | These are our four checks; whether they are the right safeguards for your risk tolerance is your call |

## The three biggest gaps, restated plainly

The table above is clause by clause. These are the three a lender should press
hardest on, because they are where a buyer gets hurt if a vendor is vague.

- **A policy engine is not a policy.** Custody now evaluates a versioned
  document and refuses what it does not approve, which is a real control and a
  real record. It has no opinion on whether the use cases you approved are the
  right ones, whether your confidence floors match your risk tolerance, or
  whether the document reflects what your business actually does. Those are the
  parts the letter is asking about, and they remain yours.
- **Custody cannot inventory models it never sees.** It knows what it was asked
  to wrap. A list assembled only from what happened to be instrumented is worse
  than no list, because it looks complete. Whatever satisfies "types of AI/ML
  used" for the whole organisation has to come from somewhere else as well.
- **The Information Security and Business Resiliency Supplement is unassessed.**
  Custody persists redacted prompts and decision outcomes, so it is squarely in
  scope of that Supplement, and it has not been evaluated against it. This is an
  open item, not a solved one.
- **No bias or fair-lending testing.** The letter asks for policies incorporating
  the characteristics of trustworthy and ethical AI/ML, and fairness is one of
  them. Custody addresses accountability, transparency, explainability and
  privacy. It does nothing whatever for fairness or harmful-bias management, and
  makes no disparate-impact claim of any kind. That obligation is real, it comes
  chiefly from ECOA and fair-lending law rather than from this letter, and
  mature vendors already serve it.

## Three design decisions worth arguing with

**Committing over a rejection is permitted, and recorded as an override.** A gate
that makes an override impossible gets bypassed entirely, and then nothing is
recorded at all. `disposition` distinguishes `committed` from
`committed_over_rejection`, so an override is visible rather than absent.

**Rejected decisions stay in the packet.** Filtering them out would produce a
cleaner artifact and a false one. An audit trail showing only the successes is a
brochure.

**A policy that permits AI to recommend but not decide can still be overridden,
and the override is recorded as `committed_without_authority`.** The alternative
is a control that makes the work impossible, which gets routed around entirely,
and then there is no record at all. The same reasoning as committing over a
rejection, and it is the second place in this library where visibility was
chosen over prevention. If your risk tolerance says otherwise, the check to add
is in your calling code, not here -- and the record will still say what
happened.
